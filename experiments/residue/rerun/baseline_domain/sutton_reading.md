# Reading a Criminal Case Record from Its Coordinates

## 1. THE READING

**What the document is.** This is most likely an official written record produced by the state in a criminal matter. My best guess is a judicial opinion or a formal statement of facts, for example a sentencing judgment, an appellate affirmance or a prosecutorial findings document, now stored in the institutional archive.

- **Form.** It asserts what happened in plain, procedural, almost contract-like language.
- **Authority.** It speaks with the full weight of the state's power to judge.
- **Function.** It tells a story ("on the night of…, the defendant…"). Secondarily, it instructs: it sets out what follows, such as sentence, remand, or denial of relief.

An alternative reading is a police or internal-affairs report that justifies an arrest or use of force. I prefer the court record because the authority here is sovereign, not merely bureaucratic, and the text is binding.

**How the document behaves.** By its own standards, the document is cooperative. It is clear, relevant, orderly and not overtly deceptive. The trouble lies in what it produces: a convincing, coherent account that may not match what happened. Its tidiness is the problem. The narrative holds together because the parts that would not fit were left out or never reached the record. It persuades because it is procedurally complete, not because it is true.

**The people involved.**

- **The subject.** An isolated individual at the margins: poor, socially disconnected, perhaps with a record, mental illness, or immigrant or minority status. They face a dense web of rules with almost no group around them. They have no organized community, no strong advocate and no counter-institution. The rules pin them in place, and nobody stands with them.
- **The others.** Police, prosecutors, a defense lawyer, witnesses and the court are entangled. Their careers, conviction records and reputations depend on the same account holding. That shared stake turns the conflict into mutual reinforcement among officials rather than a clean adversarial contest.

**How the conflict works.**

- **Suppression.** The central dynamic is suppression. Most plausibly, exculpatory evidence was withheld, an alternative suspect was ignored, or a confession was obtained under pressure and then cleaned up in the telling. It could also be the defendant's own account being procedurally excluded.
- **Rationalizing.** The record is written after the decisive events and works to justify them, not discover them.
- **Exponential escalation.** Each ruling builds on the last. Every procedural step narrows the room to reopen the case and raises the cost of admitting error for everyone involved. Charge leads to plea or conviction, then appeal denied, then the record becomes precedent for itself.

**How it felt.**

- **For the subject:** a slow, airless helplessness. Official language described them in terms they did not recognize, and each formal step made their own version less audible.
- **For the officials:** likely routine and even reassuring. The paperwork was in order.
- **For readers later:** the feeling, if it surfaces, is the jolt of realizing how reasonable the injustice sounds.

**How it resolves.** Within this act, it does not. The archive makes the account durable and authoritative. If correction comes, it comes later and from outside: an innocence project, investigative journalism, DNA testing, or a whistleblower. The society-level overlay suggests the case may eventually become a public reference point for systemic failure. Inside the document itself, though, the illusion holds.

## 2. THE RESIDUE

The coordinates cannot tell me:

- **Identifying facts:** names, jurisdiction, country, legal system, era, and the actual offence charged.
- **Document type:** whether this is truly a judgment, a police report, a prosecutorial memo or a later official review.
- **What was suppressed:** evidence, testimony, a suspect, or the defendant's voice, and who did the suppressing and whether deliberately.
- **Truth value:** whether the narrative is actually false, or only incomplete or misleading. "Illusion" registers a gap but not its size.
- **Guilt or innocence** of the subject, and the sentence or stakes.
- **The marginalized person's identity:** what exactly makes them marginal (race, class, mental health, status), and whether any allies existed.
- **Outcome:** whether exoneration, retrial, settlement, or a public scandal ever followed, and how long it took.
- **Texture and voice:** the actual wording, tone, specific claims and quoted testimony.
- **Emotional reality:** the personal histories and inner lives of everyone involved, including victims of the underlying crime, who are entirely absent from the coordinates.

## 1. The Reading

This looks like a **wrongful conviction resting on mistaken eyewitness identification**, recorded after the fact in an exoneration file, appellate opinion, or innocence-organization case summary.

**What happened structurally.** A state criminal justice apparatus (police, prosecutors, a trial court) with sovereign authority to convict and imprison built its case almost entirely from outside parties: victims and bystanders. At least four of the five elements were sincere but wrong. These were most likely the identifications of several eyewitnesses, or one victim's identification echoed by corroborating witnesses. Nobody fabricated anything and nobody coached anyone. People honestly believed they recognized the defendant and said so under oath.

The fifth element is different. It came from inside the institution and was inflated rather than invented. The most plausible version is forensic testimony, such as a hair comparison, serology, a partial print, or a "consistent with" finding, presented to the jury as stronger than the science allowed. Its role was probably to make several shaky identifications feel like convergent proof. That weak forensic piece, stacked on sincere witnesses, is the classic shape of the wrongful convictions later reversed by DNA testing.

**How it felt from inside.** The coordinates mark the resonance as an illusion. The account felt solid to everyone involved:
- Jurors heard confident, tearful, consistent witnesses, plus an expert in agreement.
- The witnesses felt certain.
- Prosecutors felt they were enforcing the law.

The cooperative norms were honored, since everyone spoke in good faith. That is exactly why the error was so durable: there was no liar to unmask. For the defendant, it was the experience of a whole institution sincerely and confidently agreeing on something false. The bureaucratic, rule-bound, high-grid setting meant the record took on a life of its own once the verdict was entered.

**The conflict's shape.** The conflict is entangled, with suppression and rationalizing. I read this as the post-conviction phase rather than the original trial:
- Appeals and new-evidence motions were met with procedural barriers, finality doctrines, and official reluctance to revisit.
- The institution explained away doubts: "witnesses were confident," "the forensic finding was accepted practice at the time."

The "drifting" terrain fits years passing, standards of forensic testimony shifting, and memories of the original case fading. The "exponential" escalation suggests that once a decisive break arrived, it compounded quickly. That break was likely DNA testing, an alternative suspect, or a review exposing the forensic overstatement. One crack led to reinvestigation, which led to media attention, which led to vacatur.

**Where I am choosing.** The coordinates could also fit a non-criminal institutional finding, such as a disciplinary or child-protection ruling based on mistaken witnesses. I favor the criminal-conviction reading because of three signals together: the sovereign authority, the multiple outside-party witness elements, and the single inflated expert element.

**Resolution.** The text itself is assertive, archival, and narrative with an instructional secondary purpose. That suggests it is written from the far side: a documented exoneration whose lesson is about eyewitness fallibility and forensic overstatement. It likely ends in formal release or reversal, but with no individual villain. The resolution is partial: the person is freed, but years are lost, accountability is diffuse, and the reform lesson outlasts any personal redress.
